### "That request took 100 ms" — fast system?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

No — one request tells you almost nothing. The same request run again gives a different time, so a single sample could
be a lucky fast one or an unlucky slow one. You can't judge a system from it.

So measure over **many** requests and summarize the spread with a few numbers →
[how are the distribution measures calculated?](#how-are-the-distribution-measures-calculated)

</details>

### How are the distribution measures calculated?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- [**Mean (average)**](#mean--strength-and-weakness) — sum all timings ÷ count.
- [**Median (p50)**](#median--strength-and-weakness) — sort fastest→slowest, take the middle value; half the requests
  are faster, half slower.
- [**High percentiles (p95, p99, p999)**](#high-percentiles--strength-and-weakness) — sort, then take the value at
  that cut. p99 = the time 99% of requests finished under; the slowest 1% took longer. (the slow end = *tail latencies*)

</details>

### Mean — strength and weakness?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- **Strength** — good for capacity math: mean × number of requests = total time spent, and
  [Little's law](3.2.2_Managing_Throughput.md#what-is-littles-law) works with averages.
- **Weakness** — it squeezes everything into one number that may match no real request. 950 requests at 100 ms + 50 at
  5 s average to ~345 ms: no request took 345 ms, and the number hides that slow requests exist at all, and how many.

</details>

### Median — strength and weakness?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- **Strength** — the honest typical wait: half the requests are faster, half slower, and a few extreme ones don't
  move it.
- **Weakness** — blind to the slow half: it can't see how much worse those requests were. Same 950 at 100 ms + 50 at
  5 s → median 100 ms, as if the 50 slow requests didn't exist.

</details>

### High percentiles — strength and weakness?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- **Strength** — they show the slow end (field term: *tail latency*). Same 950 at 100 ms + 50 at 5 s → p99 = 5 s: the
  only measure that shows the 50 slow requests. The tail is what frustrated users remember — often the biggest
  accounts, since they have the most data to process.
- **Weakness** — the deeper you go, the fewer requests sit there, so the number jumps around. Far out it is mostly
  random events you don't control, and [improving it costs more](#which-percentile-is-worth-improving).

</details>

### Which measures should judge performance?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

Each measure answers one question:

| Question                             | Measure                                                                        |
|--------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| What does a normal request get?      | [median (p50)](#median--strength-and-weakness)                                 |
| How bad is it for the slowest users? | [high percentile](#high-percentiles--strength-and-weakness) (p95 / p99 / p999) |
| How much capacity do we need?        | [mean](#mean--strength-and-weakness)                                           |

To judge performance, report **p50 and a tail percentile together** — either one alone hides the slow users or the
normal ones. The mean is for capacity math, not for judging what users feel. Pick the tail percentile by
[business value](#which-percentile-is-worth-improving).

</details>

### How to pronounce p50, p90, p99, p999, p9999?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- p50 ("p-fifty"),
- p90 ("p-ninety"),
- p99 ("p-ninety-nine"),
- p999 ("p-nine-nine-nine"),
- p9999 ("p-four-nines" or "p-nine-nine-nine-nine").

</details>

### What percentile is p999?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

The 99.9th percentile — not the 999th of anything. Extra nines push further into the slow tail:
p99 = worst 1%, p999 = worst 0.1%, p9999 = worst 0.01%.

</details>

### What does p99 < 200 ms mean?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

**p99 < 200 ms (last 5 minutes)** — in that window, 99% of requests finished in under 200 ms; the slowest 1% took
longer. (the slow end = *tail latencies*)

The value always belongs to a [time window](#how-do-you-compute-percentiles-on-live-traffic): the same service can
show 200 ms now and 2 s during a spike.

</details>

### Which percentile is worth improving?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

Pushing the slow end lower costs more the further out you go, so stop where the effort stops paying back.

- Pick the percentile by **business value** — how much a slow request there actually costs you.
- Amazon watches **p999** (slowest 1 in 1,000) because those are often the highest-spend customers.
- They stop before **p9999** (1 in 10,000): that far out, the delay comes from random events you don't control
  (a network blip, a node pause), so improving it costs a lot and buys almost nothing.

Handle: improve as deep as the money justifies; past that, you are paying to fight randomness.

</details>

### One slow call in 100 — how often?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

If a backend is slow 1% of the time (its p99), it feels rare. But a user request often waits on **many** backends,
and the slowest one delays the entire response. You roll that 1% dice for every call:

- 10 calls → 1 − 0.99¹⁰ ≈ **10%** chance of a slow response.
- 100 calls → 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ **63%** chance of a slow response.

A rare backend delay becomes the normal user experience: the backend's **p99** turns into the user's **median**.
This is known as **tail latency amplification**.

</details>

### How to stop one slow call hurting all?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

The user waits for the slowest of many calls, so shrink either the *number* of calls or the *wait* on any one:

- **Parallel, not serial:** If calls are independent, fire them concurrently. 
  The total wait drops from the *sum* of all calls to just the wait for the *slowest* call.
- **Fewer calls** — combine, use batch APIs or drop backend requests so there are fewer dice to roll.
  Fewer network calls mean fewer chances to hit a slow node.
- **Hedged Requests (Backup Requests):** If a call takes longer than the typical median (p50), fire a duplicate request
  to a different replica. Accept whichever returns first. This mathematically neutralizes the slow tail.
- **Timeout + fallback** — Cap the maximum wait. If a call breaches a strict deadline, cut it off and return cached,
  default, or partial data instead of hanging the entire response.

Handle: cut how many you wait on, and cap how long you wait on each.

</details>

### What states expected performance?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

Two ways to state expected performance/availability, often using percentiles:

- **SLO (service level objectives)** — the target itself.
  E.g. median response < 200 ms, p99 < 1 s, and ≥ 99.9% of valid requests return non-error responses.
- **SLA (service level agreements)** — a contract specifying what happens **if the SLO is not met**
  (e.g. the customer gets a refund).

So the SLO is the goal; the SLA is the goal plus consequences.

</details>

### How do you compute percentiles on live traffic?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

Keep a **rolling window** (e.g. the last 10 minutes of response times) and recompute each minute. Two ways to get the
numbers:

- **Exact** — store every response time in the window and sort it each minute. Simple, but costly in memory/CPU at
  high request volume.
- **Approximate** — feed times into a *sketch*: a small fixed-size structure that estimates percentiles cheaply.
  Names: HdrHistogram, t-digest, DDSketch. At high volume this is the normal choice.

Handle: fixed window + recompute; sort-it-all when small, sketch when the volume is large.

</details>

### How do you compute percentiles across machines?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

You have p99 from each of 10 servers and want one cluster-wide p99. **You cannot average the ten p99 values** — the
result is meaningless. A percentile depends on the *whole distribution*, not on other percentiles; server A's p99 and
server B's p99 sit at different absolute values, and their average corresponds to no real request.

Correct way: combine the **underlying data**, then take the percentile once. Each server keeps a *mergeable sketch*
(HdrHistogram, DDSketch) — you **add the sketches** into one, then read p99 off the merged result. Mergeability is the
whole reason these structures exist: it gets you a cluster percentile without shipping every raw timing to one place.

Handle: merge the data (add histograms), then compute — never average the percentiles. Same trap when coarsening time
resolution (combining minute-buckets into an hour): add the histograms, don't average.

</details>
