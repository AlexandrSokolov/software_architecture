## Nonfunctional requirements - Scalability

### What is scalability?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

A system's ability to handle **more load** by adding resources, without slowing to a crawl.

</details>

### Throughput vs scalability — difference?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- **Throughput** — a measurement: work done per second on the current setup, e.g. "2,000 requests/s on 4 machines".
  One number, so it belongs to performance, next to response time.
- **Scalability** — a property of growth: what happens to the maximum throughput as load grows and you add
  resources. Do 8 machines give ~4,000/s, or only 2,500 because they spend time keeping in sync? A curve, not a
  single number.

Trigger: throughput is how many boxes the truck delivers per hour; scalability is whether more or bigger trucks
raise that number.

</details>

### When can a system be called scalable?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

Not in general. Scalability is not a yes/no property. The word only means something once two questions are answered:
- **Scalable along which axis?** — e.g., more concurrent users, more total data, higher request rate.
- **By adding what?** — [bigger machines or more of them](3.2.2_Managing_Throughput.md#how-do-you-raise-maximum-throughput)
  (scale up vs scale out).

</details>

### Investing in scalability — what to consider?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

Match the investment to the product's stage.

Early, with few users, the priority is keeping the system **simple and flexible** so you can change features fast
as you learn what customers need. Building for hypothetical future scale is **premature optimization**:

- best case, the effort is wasted;
- worst case, it locks you into an inflexible design that's harder to evolve.

</details>

### How do you reason about scalability?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

1. [**Measure current load**](#how-do-you-describe-a-systems-load) first —
   you can't answer "what if load doubles?" without knowing today's load.
2. [**See what growth does**](#how-do-you-measure-what-happens-when-load-grows) — 
   increase load and watch what breaks or what it costs.
3. [**Judge the cost of growth**](#what-is-linear-scalability) — does handling 2× load need 2× resources, or more?

Goal throughout: **stay within the SLA while keeping cost as low as possible**.

</details>

### How do you describe a system's load?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- **Load parameters** — what to measure: a few numbers chosen for what actually stresses your specific system.
    - **Throughput** — how much per unit of time: requests/s, database writes/s, GB of new data/day.
    - **Connections / sessions** — how many clients stay connected at once: logged-in users, chat and game sessions,
      WebSockets. Each holds memory and a socket even when idle, so you size for the peak.
    - **Distribution** — what kind of load: read/write ratio, cache hit rate.
- **Average or extremes** — which cases drive the load. Twitter: if each post is copied into every follower's
  timeline, a normal user's post costs a few writes, a celebrity's post costs millions. The average follower count
  looks harmless; the few celebrities are what overload the system. So the number that matters is how followers are
  spread across users, not their average. Rule: check whether a few extreme cases carry most of the work — if they
  do, a system sized for the average breaks on them.

</details>

### How do you measure what happens when load grows?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

Once you define the [load parameters](#how-do-you-describe-a-systems-load), increase them. Two goals:

- **Find breaking points (when SLO breaks) — Fixed resources:** — Keep CPU and memory the same and add load. 
  How much does performance (response time / throughput) degrade? 
  E.g., if load doubles on the same 4 servers, does p99 go from 200 ms to 2 s?
- **Find the [cost to scale](#what-is-linear-scalability) — Fixed performance** — 
  Add load but keep response time the same. How much extra hardware must you add? 
  E.g., to keep p99 < 200 ms at double load, do you need 8 servers, or 12?

</details>

### What is linear scalability?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

**Linear scalability** — double the resources, handle double the load, same performance. This is the good case.

Rarely you do better than linear (economies of scale, better peak spreading). Far more often cost grows **faster
than linear** — e.g. with lots of data, one write can involve more work than the same write on a small dataset.

</details>

### What principles guide scalability decisions?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

Three guiding principles:

- [**No magic sauce**](#is-there-a-universal-scalable-architecture) — scalable architecture is application-specific,
  and each 10× load needs a rethink.
- [**Decompose**](#key-principle-for-building-scalable-systems) — split into parts that work independently; the hard part is where to draw the line.
- [**Stay simple**](#what-guards-against-over-engineering-scale) — don't add complexity you don't need; good designs
  are a pragmatic mix.

</details>

### Is there a "universal" scalable architecture?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

No. An architecture that scales well for one application may be a disaster for another — the design is set by the
application's specific [**load parameters**](#how-do-you-describe-a-systems-load).

Two systems can have the same total throughput (100 MB/s) but need opposite designs: one handles 100,000 tiny
requests/s (1 KB each), the other 3 huge requests/min (2 GB each).

</details>

### How far ahead should you design for scale?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

Plan for **one order of magnitude** (10×) of growth.

A design built for one load level usually won't survive 10× that load: 
at that point you rethink it and hit new bottlenecks anyway.

Designing for 100× today wastes time, delays launch, and usually guesses the future bottleneck wrong — the same trap
as [premature optimization](#investing-in-scalability--what-to-consider).

</details>

### What is the core principle for building scalable systems?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

Divide the system into smaller components that can operate **independently** with minimal coordination or shared state.
This principle drives microservices, database sharding (partitioning), and shared-nothing architectures.

The hardest engineering challenge isn't the splitting itself — it is finding the correct **boundaries**, e.g. shard by
user ID so one user's data and requests stay together. You must minimize cross-network coordination, because
components that constantly wait on each other cannot scale independently.

</details>

### What is the core principle for building scalable systems?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

The core principle is combining **decomposition** (splitting things up) with **decoupling** (minimizing dependencies).
- **Decompose** — the action: cut the system into smaller parts (services, shards, shared-nothing nodes).
- **Decouple** — the goal: each part works with minimal coordination without waiting on the others 
  or sharing state with them, so each part can grow on its own.

Split without decoupling: services that call each other on every request, or all share one database.
Separate on paper, but they can't grow separately — and now every call crosses the network.
The field calls this a **distributed monolith**.

</details>

### How do you decompose a system?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

The hard part is not the split but the **boundaries**. A good boundary gives both:
- **High cohesion** — keep together what is used together. E.g. shard by user ID: one user's data and requests stay
  on one shard, so a request never needs a second shard.
- **Low coupling** — few links across boundaries. Otherwise parts wait on each other over the network and can't grow
  separately: services that call each other on every request, or all share one database — the field calls this a
  **distributed monolith**.

One boundary decision sets both: move related things inside one part, and the links across parts disappear.

</details>

### How do you avoid over-engineering for scale?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

By optimizing for **simplicity** and avoiding *accidental complexity* — complexity that comes from your solution, not
from the problem itself. Don't add distributed-system overhead until your
[**load parameters**](#how-do-you-describe-a-systems-load) require it.

- **Prefer single-node:** If one powerful machine can handle the throughput, choose it over a distributed database.
- **Prefer manual scaling:** If your load is highly predictable, manual scaling avoids the surprises of auto-scaling.
- **Prefer fewer boundaries:** 5 services are vastly easier to monitor, deploy, and debug than 50.

The best architecture is not the most clever one; it is the simplest one that handles today's load and the
[next 10×](#how-far-ahead-should-you-design-for-scale).

</details>
