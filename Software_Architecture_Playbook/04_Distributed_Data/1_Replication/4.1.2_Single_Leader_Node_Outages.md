### Follower crash — how does it catch up?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

When a disconnected follower restarts, it resumes replication in three steps:

- **Check Local Replication Log** — the follower keeps on its disk a copy of the leader's
  [replication log](4.1.1_Single_Leader_Replication.md#recovery-log-vs-replication-log--what-for), 
  so after a restart or a network break it knows its position: the last change it got (LSN in PostgreSQL, GTID in MySQL).
- **Request Backlog** — it reconnects and asks the leader for every change after that position.
- **Apply and Follow** — once the gap is applied, it has caught up and receives new changes as before.

Handle: find where you stopped, ask for the rest, apply, keep following.

Catch-up [can fail or get costly](#follower-catch-up--what-can-go-wrong).

</details>

### Follower catch-up — what can go wrong?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- **[Costly catch-up](#long-outage--what-does-catch-up-cost)** — the leader still has the changes, but a large gap
  loads both leader and follower while it is sent and applied.
- **[Log already deleted](#replication-log-already-deleted--now-what)** — the leader deleted old replication log by
  size or age; the follower can't catch up from it and must be rebuilt from a snapshot.
- **[Leader's disk fills](#dead-follower--why-does-the-leaders-disk-fill)** — the leader keeps the replication log
  until every follower confirms; a follower that never comes back blocks deletion until the disk is full and the
  leader stops.

Handle: it depends on how long the leader keeps its replication log: kept → costly gap; deleted → rebuild; kept for
a follower that never returns → full disk.

</details>

### Long outage — what does catch-up cost?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

The follower was away long, so the gap is large. Catching it up loads both sides, on top of normal traffic:

- **Leader** — reads the old part of its replication log from disk and sends it over the network while still serving
  its normal writes. Its clients see slower responses.
- **Follower** — applies the whole gap at full speed, so its disk and CPU are busy. Until it is done, any reads it
  serves return old data.

</details>

### Replication log rotation — what are the options?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

The leader writes its replication log as a chain of files: when one is full, a new one starts, and old files are
removed whole (PostgreSQL WAL segments, MySQL binlog files). Which old files may go is set by the **retention** rule:

- **By size or age** — keep the last N GB or N days (e.g. PostgreSQL `wal_keep_size`, MySQL
  `binlog_expire_logs_seconds`). Disk use has a fixed limit, but a follower away longer finds
  [its missing changes gone](#followers-missing-changes-gone--now-what).
- **Until every follower confirms** (e.g. PostgreSQL replication slots) — no follower misses changes. But a follower
  that is dead for good, or cut off for days, doesn't confirm: the log grows until the disk is full, and the leader
  stops — no more writes. An admin must drop the dead follower's slot so old log can go again.
- **Until confirmed, with a size cap** (PostgreSQL 13+: `max_slot_wal_keep_size`) — like the rule above, but past the
  cap the leader deletes the log anyway. You give up the follower to save the leader: it must be
  [rebuilt](#followers-missing-changes-gone--now-what).

Any rule can **archive** first: old files are copied to cheap storage (e.g. S3) before deletion, so the leader's disk
stays small and the old log is still there for restores.

</details>

### Follower's missing changes gone — now what?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

The follower asks for every change after its position, but the leader already deleted that part of its replication
log under its [retention rule](#replication-log-rotation--what-are-the-options). The gap can't be filled from the log.

Fix — rebuild it like a [new follower](4.1.1_Single_Leader_Replication.md#new-follower--how-to-get-a-correct-copy):
its old data is thrown away and replaced by a fresh copy of the leader's data.

</details>

### Sync follower dies — what happens to writes?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

The leader tells the client "write OK" only after the sync follower confirms it has the write. 
That follower is gone, so no confirmation comes, and every new write hangs.

Fix: don't depend on one machine. List several followers as candidates for the sync role (the rest stay async). 
When the sync one dies, another candidate takes the role once it has caught up, and writes go on. With
[no candidate left](#no-sync-follower-left--wait-or-go-async), the database must either keep writes waiting or drop
to async.

</details>

### No sync follower left — wait or go async?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

When the last synchronous follower goes offline, the leader must choose between two fail-safes:

- **Keep writes waiting (Strict Durability):** PostgreSQL's default. The leader blocks and refuses to confirm new
  writes until a sync follower returns. 
  - Pro: Every confirmed write is guaranteed to be on two machines. 
  - Con: The database is completely down for writes until the network or follower recovers.
- **Fall back to async (High Availability):** MySQL semi-sync default (after a timeout). The leader resumes
  accepting writes and confirming them to the client immediately. 
  - Pro: The system stays up for writes. 
  - Con: If the leader crashes before followers catch up, confirmed writes are permanently lost.

**Handle:** 
- Wait when losing a write is worse than downtime (payments); 
- fall back when staying up matters more.

This is the classic trade-off between strict durability and write availability.

</details>

### Leader crash — how to resolve?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

**Failover** — promote a follower to be the new leader. It is a three-step chain:

- **[Detect](#leader-dead--how-do-you-know)** — decide that the leader is dead.
- **Choose** — [who decides](#new-leader--who-decides) and [which follower](#new-leader--which-follower) becomes the
  new leader.
- **[Reconfigure](#after-failover--who-must-switch)** — clients, other followers, and the old leader switch to the
  new leader.

Done by an admin, or automatically — faster, but [with its own risks](#failover--what-can-go-wrong). 

Handle: notice it's dead, pick the replacement, point everyone at it.

</details>

### Leader dead — how do you know?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

You can't know for sure. A dead leader, a slow leader, and a broken network look the same: no answer arrives. 
So nodes send each other small "are you alive?" messages (heartbeats) and use a timeout: 
no answer from the leader for, say, 30 s → treat it as dead.

How long to wait is [a trade-off](#failover--what-can-go-wrong).

</details>

### Planned leader change — how?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

A **Switchover** is a graceful, admin-triggered handover (e.g., for maintenance), meaning there is no "Detect" step
and no data loss.

- **Stop accepting new writes** on the old leader.
- **Wait** until the chosen follower has applied every change — its position equals the leader's.
- **Promote** the follower to the new leader, switch clients to it, and make the old leader a follower.

**Handle:**
Switchover skips detection; 
Cost: writes pause for a few seconds. Field term: **switchover**.

</details>

### New leader — who decides?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- **Election** — the remaining replicas vote; a candidate needs a majority (more than half). 
- **Controller node** — a node chosen earlier for this job appoints the new leader.

Either way, all nodes must agree on exactly one leader — a consensus problem.

</details>

### New leader — which follower?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

The most up-to-date one: it has the most of the old leader's changes, so the least data is lost.

- **Sync or semi-sync** — the follower the leader waited for; it has every confirmed write.
- **Async** — The follower with the highest position (e.g., highest LSN in PostgreSQL or GTID in MySQL) wins the election. 

Even the best async follower may miss the leader's last writes; those [are lost](#failover--what-can-go-wrong).

</details>

### After failover — who must switch?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- **Clients** — send writes to the new leader. Usually a DNS name, a proxy, or a shared IP address is moved to point 
  at it.
- **Other followers** — read the replication log from the new leader.
- **Old leader** — if it comes back, it must become a follower. If it still thinks it is the leader, there are
  [two leaders](#failover--what-can-go-wrong).

</details>

### Failover — what can go wrong?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- **[Lost writes](#async-failover--which-writes-are-lost)** — with async replication, the old leader's last writes
  never reach the new leader and are thrown away; other systems may still point at them.
- **[Two leaders](#two-leaders--how-to-prevent-it)** — the old leader still thinks it leads; both accept writes and
  data is lost or corrupted.
- **[Wrong timeout](#failover-timeout--how-long)** — 
  - too long → longer downtime; 
  - too short → failover when the leader was fine.

Because of these, some teams [fail over by hand](#failover--automatic-or-manual).

Handle: failover acts on a guess — the new leader may lack writes, the old one may be alive, the guess may come too
early or too late.

</details>

### Async failover — which writes are lost?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

The old leader's last writes — confirmed to clients, but not yet sent to any follower. Each step causes the next:

- **New leader lacks them** — it was promoted without those writes.
- **Old leader drops them** — when it comes back, its log has changes the new leader never got. It cuts its log back
  to the last change both share before it can follow (PostgreSQL: `pg_rewind`). Writes the client was told are OK are
  gone.
- **IDs are reused** — the new leader's ID counter is behind, so it hands out IDs the old leader already used. Other
  systems that stored the old IDs now point at the wrong rows. Real case (GitHub): the IDs were also keys in a Redis
  store, so some private data was shown to the wrong users.

Fixes:

- **Stop the loss** — sync or semi-sync replication, so at least one follower has every confirmed write; promote
  [the most up-to-date follower](#new-leader--which-follower).
- **Stop the damage** — IDs that can't repeat: UUIDs, a separate ID generator, or move the counter forward after
  failover.

</details>

### Two leaders — how to prevent it?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

The old leader was cut off, not dead, and still thinks it leads. Now two nodes accept writes, nothing merges them, and
data is lost or corrupted. Field term: **split brain**.

Ways to stop the old leader, used alone or together:

- **Fencing (External enforcement)** — something outside stops it:
  - **Infrastructure Fencing (STONITH):** — Shut it down — A cluster manager sends a command to a smart power switch or 
    hypervisor to physically cut the old leader's power or network access.
  - **Storage Fencing (Fencing Tokens):** — Reject its writes — every new leader a higher generation number (token). 
    The storage refuses writes that carry an old number.
- **Step down (Internal enforcement)** — it stops itself: the leader keeps checking 
  that it can reach a majority of nodes (a quorum); when it can't, it stops taking writes.

Handle: stopped from outside (fencing) or stops itself (no majority).

</details>

### Failover timeout — how long?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- **Too long** — the leader is really dead, but no one takes over until the timeout ends: writes are down all that time.
- **Too short** — a short network glitch or load spike looks like death. The failover was not needed (a false positive), 
  and it adds work — catch-up, redirects — to a system that is already overloaded.

Pick it from measured normal response times, and wait for several missed heartbeats rather than one.

Handle: false positive vs longer downtime.

</details>

### Failover — automatic or manual?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- **Automatic** — takes over in seconds, but acts on a guess: it can
  [fail over when the leader was fine](#failover-timeout--how-long) or cause
  [two leaders](#two-leaders--how-to-prevent-it).
- **Manual** — an admin is alerted and decides. Slower (minutes, maybe at 3 a.m.), but a human checks that the leader
  is really dead and picks the follower.

Manual wins when a wrong failover would do more damage than a few minutes of downtime; automatic wins when every
minute down is expensive and fencing is in place.

</details>
