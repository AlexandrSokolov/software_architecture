### Follower crash — how does it catch up?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- **Check Local Log** — each follower keeps a log on its disk of the changes it got from the leader, so after a restart
  or a network break it knows the last change it applied.
- **Request Backlog** — it reconnects and asks the leader for every change after that point.
- **Apply and Follow** — once the gap is applied, it has caught up and receives new changes as before.

Limits:
- **Performance** — after a long outage or with many writes, the backlog is large: high load on both follower and
  leader.
- **The Leader's Dilemma** — the leader deletes its log only after all followers have confirmed it. 
  If a follower stays away long, the leader must choose: keep the log and risk filling its disk, or delete it — 
  then the follower can't catch up and must be rebuilt from a backup.

</details>

### Leader crash — how to resolve?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

**Failover**: promote a follower to leader, send clients' writes to it, and make the other followers follow it. 
Done by an admin, or automatically — [which has its own risks](#failover--what-can-go-wrong):
- **Detect** — no reliable way to know what happened, so a timeout: no answer from the leader for, say, 30 s → dead.
  Planned maintenance skips this: the leader hands over before shutting down.
- **Choose** — the remaining replicas elect a new leader by majority, or a controller node appoints one. 
  Pick the most up-to-date follower to lose the least data: 
  - the one the leader waited for (sync, semi-sync), or 
  - the one with the highest log sequence number (async). 
  Agreeing on one leader is a consensus problem.
- **Reconfigure** — clients write to the new leader; if the old leader comes back, it must become a follower.

</details>

### Failover — what can go wrong?
<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

- **Lost writes** — with async replication, the new leader may miss the old leader's last writes. 
  When the old leader returns, those writes are usually discarded: confirmed writes weren't durable after all.
- **External System Inconsistency** — Discarding writes can break external systems. 
  For example, if a new leader reuses discarded auto-incrementing IDs, 
  it can cause catastrophic data mismatches with external caches (like Redis).
- **Split brain** — A network partition might cause two nodes to believe they are the leader and both take writes.
  If both accept writes, data is corrupted. Systems must implement "fencing" to shut down the old leader.
- **Timeout choice** — 
  - If the failure timeout is too long, the system stays down longer. 
  - If it's too short, a temporary network glitch or load spike might trigger an unnecessary failover, 
    making an overloaded system worse.

No easy fix, so some teams fail over manually even when the software can do it automatically.

</details>
